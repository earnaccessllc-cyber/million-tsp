FERS High-3 Salary: What Counts for USPS and Federal Workers?

A federal employee's biggest paycheck is not necessarily the salary used to calculate a retirement pension. For USPS letter carriers especially, overtime, penalty overtime, and other premiums can make gross pay much higher than the basic pay that counts in a FERS high-3 average. This MillionFed guide explains the distinction, how the three-year period works, and how to avoid overstating a pension estimate.

Quick answer: FERS high-3 is the highest time-weighted average of retirement-creditable basic pay during three consecutive years of creditable service. For USPS FERS employees, the Postal Service's ELM §586.252 expressly includes qualifying higher-level pay but excludes overtime, night differential, allowances, premium pay, and lump-sum terminal leave. See USPS ELM 586.25 and OPM's FERS computation guidance.

What is the FERS high-3 average salary?

Your high-3 is not the average of your three highest annual W-2 totals. It is an average of rates of basic pay over the three consecutive years of creditable service that produce the largest result. If your pay rate changes partway through a year, each rate is weighted by how long it applied. The best period is often the last 36 months, but an earlier three-year period can win if you previously held a higher-paying position.

OPM's CSRS/FERS Handbook, Chapter 50 describes the time-weighted calculation. USPS ELM 586.241–586.251 likewise says the period need not start on the first of a month or on a pay-change date. A break in service has specific consecutive-service treatment; do not assume it automatically makes every earlier high-pay period unusable.

The controlling USPS source is ELM 586.252, Basic Pay. Its language is particularly useful because general federal retirement articles sometimes mix rules for General Schedule employees with Postal Service pay rules. If a pay item has an unfamiliar name, ask USPS benefits or payroll whether it is retirement-creditable basic pay before adding it to a high-3 estimate.

Does overtime help a letter carrier's pension?

Not directly through high-3. Suppose a carrier's retirement-creditable basic salary is $84,000 and the carrier earns an additional $20,000 in overtime during a year. Gross pay from those two items would be $104,000, but the overtime does not turn the high-3 rate into $104,000. The carrier may have more cash to save in the TSP or elsewhere; that is a separate effect, not an increase in the FERS pension salary base.

The same principle applies to working extra hours shortly before retirement. A year of unusually high overtime may be financially useful, but it does not substitute for an increase in retirement-creditable basic pay. Avoid using the total earnings box on a pay statement as a high-3 input without separating the pay categories.

How is higher-level USPS pay treated?

USPS ELM specifically says basic pay for retirement purposes includes higher-level pay. That is different from an overtime premium. However, not every extra assignment or informal responsibility creates qualifying higher-level pay. The actual personnel/payroll record, effective dates, and whether the payment was classified as higher-level basic pay determine the amount and period that can enter the average.

For example, if an eligible employee's retirement-creditable rate is temporarily higher for four months, the high-3 calculation weights that higher rate for those four months rather than treating it as if it applied for all three years. Get the official salary history from USPS HR before assuming a temporary detail materially raises the pension.

What about locality pay for other federal employees?

For many General Schedule employees, applicable locality pay is included in the retirement-creditable basic-pay rate. The OPM FERS computation page defines high-3 using basic pay, and OPM's retirement rules account for the basic-pay components that are creditable under the employee's pay system. But USPS career letter carriers do not simply add a GS locality percentage to their negotiated postal salary. Use the pay rules for your actual position, not a generic GS calculator.

Other federal occupations can have special basic-pay provisions, such as certain law-enforcement availability pay or wage-system differentials. This guide's USPS table is not a universal list for every federal job. Check the governing pay authority and official retirement estimate for unusual compensation.

Worked high-3 example: three salary rates

Assume the highest consecutive three-year period contains exactly one full year at each of these retirement-creditable annual basic-pay rates. This simplified example assumes each rate applied for a full year:

YearAnnual basic-pay rateWeight
Year 1$80,0001 year
Year 2$84,0001 year
Year 3$88,0001 year

High-3 = ($80,000 + $84,000 + $88,000) ÷ 3 = $84,000. The final $88,000 rate does not replace the earlier rates. If the employee received a raise halfway through a year, the calculation would use the appropriate fractions of each rate instead of these full-year weights. OPM's exact calculation uses official service and pay dates.

What would an $84,000 high-3 mean for a FERS pension?

For an ordinary FERS employee retiring at 57 with 35 years of creditable service and an unreduced immediate pension, the basic formula is $84,000 × 35 × 1% = $29,400 a year, or $2,450 per month before survivor reductions, taxes, and other adjustments. This is an illustration, not an estimate for a specific USPS employee.

At age 62 or older with at least 20 years of service at retirement, the general FERS multiplier is 1.1%. At $84,000 high-3 and 20 years, the illustration would be $18,480 annually before reductions. An ordinary letter carrier retiring at 57 does not get the 1.1% multiplier simply because they have more than 20 years. OPM's FERS computation rules distinguish the two cases. Special-category employees and disability annuitants can have different calculations.

How much does a higher high-3 matter? At 35 years and a 1% multiplier, each additional $1,000 in the high-3 adds $350 per year to the unreduced basic annuity. A $6,000 increase that applies only to the last one of three full years would raise this simplified high-3 by $2,000, not $6,000, if that three-year period remains the highest. That would add $700 annually at 35 years and 1%.

What if the last three years were not your best?

Imagine an employee moved from a higher-paid position to a lower-paid position shortly before retirement. An earlier consecutive three-year period might produce the largest average. OPM and USPS both require the highest qualifying period, not automatically the final three years. The periods must still meet the consecutive-service rule; you cannot select three disconnected years solely because each had a high rate.

Pay changes, part-time schedules, breaks in service, refunded service, and special retirement coverage can make the actual computation more complex. OPM's Chapter 50 handbook provides the official methodology. For part-time service, the salary averaging and service-proration rules need to be considered separately; do not just multiply a reduced paycheck by three.

How to check your high-3 before retirement

  1. Obtain your official USPS or agency retirement estimate and the salary history used to prepare it.
  2. Identify each retirement-creditable basic-pay rate and the effective dates during the likely highest three-year period.
  3. Separate overtime, night differential, awards, and other excluded items from the basic-pay rates.
  4. Ask HR to review qualifying higher-level pay, a previous higher-paying assignment, part-time service, or a break in service if applicable.
  5. Use the verified high-3 with your creditable years and the correct FERS multiplier, then account separately for survivor elections and other reductions.

For USPS employees, the ELM's FERS annuity computation section is the starting point. The agency estimate is preliminary; OPM determines the final annuity when processing the retirement application.

High-3, the FERS supplement, and TSP are three different numbers

High-3 affects the basic FERS annuity. The FERS Special Retirement Supplement uses a different calculation tied to Social Security-covered FERS service, not a simple percentage of your high-3. TSP balances and withdrawals are separate from both. An employee could have a large TSP balance and modest high-3, or a high-3 salary and a smaller TSP account. Frequently asked questions

Does USPS penalty overtime count in high-3?

No. The USPS rule excludes overtime from retirement basic pay. A larger gross paycheck from penalty overtime does not make the retirement-creditable annual salary rate larger.

Can a temporary higher-level assignment count?

Qualifying higher-level pay is included under USPS ELM §586.252, weighted by its effective duration. Confirm the official pay classification and dates; a temporary assignment does not automatically raise the rate for the full 36 months.

Do the three years have to be calendar years?

No. USPS ELM §586.241 says the period need not begin on the first day of a month or a pay-change date. OPM uses the three consecutive years that produce the highest average.

Is a $100,000 W-2 the same as a $100,000 high-3?

No. The W-2 reflects taxable pay and can include compensation excluded from the retirement basic-pay calculation. High-3 uses eligible basic-pay rates over time.

Does unused sick leave increase high-3?

No. For an eligible retirement, unused sick leave can add service credit for annuity computation; it does not raise the high-3 salary rate. See MillionFed's unused sick leave guide.
