TSP Loans After Leaving Federal Service: Repayment and Tax Rules

A federal employee can leave government service while still owing money on a Thrift Savings Plan loan. That does not automatically mean the entire loan becomes taxable on the retirement date. But payroll deductions stop, and missing the TSP's post-separation repayment instructions can turn an outstanding balance into a taxable loan offset. Here's how the rules work for federal employees, including regular USPS letter carriers.

Quick answer: After separation, TSP regulations allow an outstanding loan to be repaid in full or continued through permitted post-separation payment methods. If you do not begin payments or pay off the loan by the deadline stated by the TSP record keeper, the remaining principal and accrued interest can be offset against your TSP account and reported to the IRS. The deadline is set by the actual notice, not a universal 90-day rule. See 5 CFR Part 1655.

What changes when you retire or resign?

While employed, TSP loan installments normally come through payroll deduction. When you separate, that payroll mechanism ends. Under 5 CFR §1655.14, a separated participant with an outstanding loan can continue repayments through approved methods, including loan direct debit, or pay off the balance. The record keeper communicates the applicable deadline and repayment instructions.

Retirement, resignation, and another separation from federal service can all create this issue. The loan rules are distinct from the rules for starting a FERS pension, qualifying for the FERS Special Retirement Supplement (SRS), or taking an ordinary TSP withdrawal.

Three possible outcomes

What happensGeneral consequence
Pay the loan off under TSP instructionsThe outstanding debt is satisfied; the payoff itself is not an ordinary TSP withdrawal.
Continue timely post-separation paymentsThe loan generally remains outstanding under its repayment terms; missed payments must be cured as required.
Fail to start payments or repay by the stated deadlineThe TSP may offset the unpaid loan and accrued interest against the account, with potential income-tax consequences.

Do not assume the TSP will automatically withdraw your loan payment from your FERS annuity. Check your TSP account messages, current repayment settings, and payment confirmation after separation.

What is a TSP loan offset?

A loan offset means the plan reduces your retirement account balance to satisfy an unpaid loan. It is not the same as the TSP sending you a new cash payment. Nevertheless, the IRS treats an offset as an actual distribution for federal tax purposes. For traditional TSP amounts, the portion not successfully rolled over can be included in taxable income; an additional 10% early-distribution tax may apply unless an exception applies. IRS: Plan loan offsets.

For example, suppose a participant has a $12,000 unpaid traditional TSP loan when a qualifying offset occurs. The account balance is reduced by the offset amount. Without an eligible rollover, the $12,000 may be taxable in the year of the offset. This is a simplified illustration; it is not a prediction of the person's tax bill or an assertion that all loans have the same tax treatment.

Loan offset versus deemed distribution: why the difference matters

These two terms are easy to confuse. A deemed distribution can arise when a loan fails to meet federal repayment requirements, potentially while the participant is still employed. A deemed distribution is generally taxable but is not eligible for rollover. A plan loan offset actually reduces the plan balance and may be eligible for rollover. The IRS loan FAQ explicitly distinguishes them. If you previously missed payments, verify whether the loan was already deemed distributed before assuming the more favorable offset rules apply.

Can you roll over an unpaid TSP loan offset?

Potentially, but you generally need to contribute replacement money from another source to an eligible retirement account: the loan balance was not paid out to you in cash at the time of offset. Under IRS rules, a qualified plan loan offset caused by qualifying separation from employment can have an extended rollover deadline: the due date, including extensions, for the federal income-tax return for the year the offset occurs. An offset that does not meet the qualified criteria may have a different, generally shorter rollover deadline. See IRS guidance on qualified plan loan offsets and Form 5329 instructions.

The extended deadline does not mean the TSP must accept a late loan payment, and it does not make an offset tax-free by itself. It describes a potential tax rollover of the offset amount into an eligible retirement plan, subject to the applicable requirements. Get the exact distribution classification and date from TSP records before taking tax action.

What if you miss a payment after separation?

Under the TSP regulations, participants who begin post-separation repayment and then miss an installment receive instructions to make up missed payments. If missed payments are not cured by the end of the calendar quarter following the quarter of the first missed payment, the record keeper can declare a loan offset. That rule is different from the initial deadline to start repayments after separation, which is communicated in the record keeper's notice. Review §1655.14(g) and §1655.15.

USPS example: retiring at 57 with a loan

Consider a regular USPS city letter carrier who retires at age 57 while still repaying a TSP loan. The USPS payroll deductions end at separation, but the TSP loan can generally be repaid using the TSP's post-separation process. If it instead becomes a taxable loan offset, the IRS's separation-from-service exception to the additional 10% early-distribution tax may apply because the carrier separated during or after the year they turned 55. That does not automatically remove ordinary income tax. Read MillionFed's TSP rule of 55 guide for the precise timing test.

Regular USPS letter carriers should not assume they qualify for the separate special public-safety tax exception simply because their work is physically demanding. And a TSP loan offset does not change the separate FERS pension or SRS eligibility requirements. See the FERS retirement eligibility guide.

Checklist before your final federal paycheck

  1. Open your TSP account and confirm each loan's outstanding principal, accrued interest, and repayment schedule.
  2. Check the TSP record keeper's separation notice and exact initial repayment deadline.
  3. Decide whether to continue permitted repayments or pay off the loan, based on your own circumstances.
  4. Verify your bank payment arrangement and keep transaction confirmations; do not assume payroll deduction will continue.
  5. Ask the TSP how any previous missed payments affect the loan's status.
  6. If an offset is reported, retain your Form 1099-R and ask a qualified tax professional whether the offset qualifies for rollover or an early-distribution exception.
  7. Coordinate any loan decision with your FERS retirement application checklist and household cash needs.

Common questions

Does retiring immediately cancel my TSP loan?

No. TSP regulations permit post-separation repayment. An offset can follow if you fail to comply with the applicable notice and repayment terms.

Is there always a 90-day deadline?

No. Do not rely on a generic 90-day statement. The record keeper's notice controls the initial post-separation deadline; separate regulatory cure rules apply after a participant begins payments and then misses an installment.

Will an offset affect my FERS pension amount?

A TSP loan offset affects the TSP account and potential taxes, not the basic FERS annuity formula itself. Your pension calculation follows separate OPM rules.
