TSP G Fund vs. C Fund: Safety, Risk and Growth

Safety versus growth in the TSP, and how to decide how much of each you need.

Updated November 17, 2026 · MillionFed Guides

The G Fund and C Fund serve different roles. The G Fund holds special U.S. Treasury securities backed by the federal government, protecting principal from investment loss but not from inflation. The C Fund tracks the S&P 500 Index, which includes roughly 500 large U.S. companies and can lose value when stocks fall. The right allocation depends on when you need the money, your other retirement income, and how much market risk you can tolerate. See the USDA National Finance Center TSP fund overview.

The case for the G Fund

The G Fund is unusual: its special-issue Treasury securities provide principal protection from investment loss. That can help limit volatility in a retirement portfolio. But principal protection is not the same as inflation protection; if prices rise faster than the G Fund earns interest, the real purchasing power of those dollars falls. The federal TSP plan summary distinguishes the G Fund from the F, C, S and I Funds, which can incur investment losses. Choosing how much to hold in G is a risk-management decision, not a universal retirement formula.

The case for the C Fund

The C Fund seeks to match the performance of the S&P 500, a broad index of large U.S. companies. Stocks have historically offered growth potential over long periods, but future returns and recovery dates are not guaranteed. A large market decline near retirement can materially affect a withdrawal plan, and an investor may have to sell at a loss to meet expenses. The USDA National Finance Center describes the C Fund as an S&P 500 index investment, not a guaranteed savings product.

The biggest mistake: switching after a crash

Changing investments during a market decline can lock in losses and may leave an investor out of a later rebound. But the opposite extreme—assuming every decline will recover before you need the money—is also risky. Decide on a diversified allocation and a withdrawal plan you can maintain through bad markets, and revisit it when your retirement horizon or finances change. No fund mix eliminates investment risk or inflation risk.

A simple way to think about the split

  • Near-term spending needs: consider how much principal stability matters. Unlike G, the F Fund can lose value.
  • Longer investment horizon: consider whether exposure to stocks such as the C, S and I Funds fits your tolerance for large losses.
  • Prefer a preset mix: a TSP Lifecycle (L) Fund adjusts its allocation over time, but it can still lose money.

Questions federal employees ask about G vs. C

Is the G Fund safer than the C Fund?

For avoiding investment losses of principal, yes. The G Fund is protected against that specific risk. It still faces inflation risk, while the C Fund can lose market value. “Safer” depends on whether you mean short-term stability or preserving purchasing power over many years.

Can the C Fund lose money in a single year?

Yes. Its value follows stock-market movements, and losses are possible even if you hold it for many years. Past long-term growth does not guarantee future results or the timing of a recovery.

Should I move all my TSP into G when I retire?

Not automatically. Retirement does not require a single fund allocation. Consider planned withdrawals, pension and Social Security income, emergency savings, risk tolerance, and inflation before making changes. See MillionFed’s full TSP funds guide and traditional vs. Roth withdrawal tax guide.
