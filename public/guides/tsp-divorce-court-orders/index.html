TSP and Divorce: How Federal Retirement Court Orders Work

Dividing a federal employee's Thrift Savings Plan in a divorce takes more than a handshake or a generic retirement-account clause. This MillionFed guide explains the federal TSP Court Order Center's process, restrictions, and practical pitfalls without promising a particular legal outcome.

Quick answer: The TSP must determine that a submitted domestic-relations court order qualifies under its own rules. During review, restrictions may prevent TSP loans and withdrawals. Your FERS pension and TSP are separate benefits.

A divorce decree and a TSP court order are not the same thing

A Thrift Savings Plan (TSP) account is a federal retirement account, not a state-law bank account. A divorce, legal separation, or child-support proceeding may lead to a court order awarding a portion of a participant's TSP benefit to a spouse, former spouse, child, or other qualifying payee. But the TSP's Court Order Center must review the order and determine whether it qualifies under TSP requirements. Simply agreeing to split an account in a settlement does not itself establish that the TSP has accepted an enforceable order. See the TSP Court Order Overview.

What is a Retirement Benefits Court Order?

A Retirement Benefits Court Order (RBCO) can recognize the right of a spouse, former spouse, child, or other dependent to part of a TSP account as a result of a domestic-relations proceeding. The TSP separately recognizes Child Support Court Orders (CSCOs). The terminology and qualification process differ from private-sector qualified domestic relations orders (QDROs). Do not assume a generic QDRO template will meet the federal plan's requirements. The TSP's qualification requirements specify information and conditions that an order must satisfy.

What details must the order contain?

The TSP's published checklist calls for a properly issued, certified order identifying the Thrift Savings Plan, the participant and payee, the applicable domestic-relations basis, and a clear description of the award and how it is calculated. The order must not require the plan to provide a benefit the plan does not offer or to pay more than the participant is entitled to. Sensitive identification details can be supplied in an appropriate separate attachment rather than published in a public court filing, subject to the Court Order Center's instructions. Have a qualified attorney review the proposed order where practical. Read the official requirements.

What happens after a court order is submitted?

According to the TSP Court Order Center, a court-certified order can be uploaded or mailed for review. The center reviews whether it qualifies and sends a written determination. Its guidance describes a 20-day review period after receipt, but complex cases may take longer; do not plan around a guaranteed payment date. If the order qualifies, the center directs the TSP recordkeeper to establish the applicable award. If it is denied, the notice explains the reason and a revised order may be submitted.

Can you withdraw from your TSP during the review?

Potentially not. The Court Order Center states that receiving a draft domestic-relations order, court order, or joinder can trigger restrictions on the participant's benefit activity. While the restriction applies, distributions, loans, and withdrawals are not allowed; investment choices and contribution amounts may still be changed. This matters if you are separating from federal service, planning to use TSP money for expenses, or anticipating a rollover. See TSP benefit restrictions.

Does a court order change the beneficiary designation?

Do not assume that dividing the TSP automatically updates who receives the participant's remaining account balance at death. The TSP's court-order procedures explain that an order may direct a participant to name a particular beneficiary, but the participant must complete the relevant designation. Separately review beneficiary elections in your official TSP account and confirm any required changes have been accepted. A former spouse's rights under an accepted court order and a beneficiary designation are distinct issues.

What about taxes and rollover options?

The tax treatment of a payment under an approved court order depends on who receives it, the type of TSP money, the form of payment, and applicable federal tax rules. A transfer under a divorce order should not be treated as automatically tax-free or penalty-free without checking the precise circumstances. For federal employees, consult the IRS Publication 721, the TSP's payment instructions, and an appropriately qualified tax adviser before choosing how to receive or move funds. This article does not calculate any person's tax liability.

USPS and other FERS employees: what is different?

The TSP court-order framework generally applies to both USPS letter carriers and other civilian federal employees participating in the TSP. Your FERS basic pension, any FERS survivor annuity, and the TSP are separate benefits with separate rules. A court order addressing one benefit does not necessarily dispose of the others. For the beneficiary side, see MillionFed’s TSP beneficiary guide. For the pension side, read MillionFed's FERS survivor benefit options guide. For TSP withdrawals after leaving service, see the TSP rule-of-55 guide. Special-category retirement rules should not be imported into the TSP court-order qualification process.

A practical checklist before signing an agreement

Confirm the order names the correct plan and account type; confirm how the award is described; review the TSP's qualification requirements; ask whether any draft or submitted order will restrict loans and withdrawals; verify where the certified order must be sent; separately review your beneficiary designation; and get individualized advice about taxes and related FERS pension provisions. Keep copies of the court-certified order and the TSP's written determination. The TSP Court Order Center's official FAQs are a useful starting point, but cannot replace legal advice for a contested divorce.

Frequently asked questions

Is a divorce decree enough to split my TSP?

Not necessarily. The Court Order Center must determine that the submitted order meets the TSP's requirements before the plan administers an award.

Can a former spouse receive part of my TSP?

Yes, when a qualifying domestic-relations court order awards a share and the TSP accepts the order.

Can I take a TSP loan while a court order is being reviewed?

A restriction triggered by an order can prevent loans, withdrawals, and distributions until the restriction is removed. Check the status with the TSP.

Is the FERS pension divided by the same TSP order?

Do not assume so. The FERS annuity and the TSP are separate benefits and have different administrative requirements.
