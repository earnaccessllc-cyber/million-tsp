How to become a TSP millionaire

The match, the limits and the math behind a $1 million Thrift Savings Plan balance.

Updated October 27, 2026 · MillionFed Guides

A $1 million TSP balance is reachable on a federal salary. It takes three things: contributing enough to get the full agency match, starting early, and leaving the money invested long enough for growth to compound.

Step 1: Never leave the agency match on the table

Under FERS, your agency puts money into your TSP for you:

  • 1% automatic contribution, even if you contribute nothing.
  • Dollar-for-dollar match on the first 3% of pay you contribute.
  • 50 cents per dollar on the next 2%.

Contribute 5% and your agency adds another 5%, so 10% of your pay goes in each paycheck. Contributing less than 5% means turning down free money.

Step 2: Know the contribution limits

2026 IRS limitAmount
Employee contributions (traditional + Roth)$24,500
Catch-up, age 50 and older+$8,000
Higher catch-up, ages 60 to 63+$11,250

Agency contributions do not count toward your limit. The IRS adjusts these figures most years, so check tsp.gov for the current year.

Step 3: How much you need to save each year

Assuming a 7% average annual return, which is an illustration and not a promise, here is the total yearly amount (your contributions plus your agency's) needed to reach $1 million:

Years until retirementNeeded per yearAbout per month
35 years$7,234$603
30 years$10,586$882
25 years$15,811$1,318
20 years$24,393$2,033

The pattern is the point: waiting 15 years more than triples the yearly amount required. Starting early does more of the work than any fund choice.

Example: a GS employee earning $75,000 who contributes 10% puts in $7,500 a year. The agency adds 5%, or $3,750. That $11,250 a year is enough to reach roughly $1 million in about 30 years at a 7% average return, before counting raises.

Step 4: Pick a growth-oriented mix and stay with it

Reaching seven figures in 25 to 35 years depends on stock-market growth. Historically the C, S and I funds have grown faster than the G and F funds over long periods, with much bigger short-term swings. Many people who reach $1 million kept contributing through downturns instead of moving everything to the G Fund after a crash, which locks in losses.

Step 5: Raise your rate with every raise

Each time you get a step increase or annual pay raise, move your contribution up by 1%. You never see a smaller paycheck, and the extra money compounds for the rest of your career.

Traditional or Roth?

Traditional TSP contributions lower your taxes now; Roth contributions are taxed now but qualified withdrawals are tax free. Many younger employees in lower tax brackets favor Roth. Agency contributions always go into the traditional balance.
