Traditional vs. Roth TSP Withdrawals in Retirement: 2026 Rules

A federal employee can retire with traditional and Roth money inside the same Thrift Savings Plan account. Yet those two balances do not have the same tax treatment. Knowing the difference can prevent an expensive surprise, especially when you leave federal service before age 59½ or consider a Roth conversion. This MillionFed guide covers the withdrawal rules in effect in 2026, including an important change that older retirement articles may miss.

Quick answer: Traditional TSP withdrawals are generally taxable as ordinary income. Roth TSP contributions have already been taxed, but Roth earnings are tax-free only if the distribution meets the qualified-distribution rules. The TSP also began offering eligible in-plan Roth conversions on January 28, 2026. See IRS Publication 721 and the TSP's 2026 conversion announcement.

Traditional and Roth TSP: what actually gets taxed?

IssueTraditional TSPRoth TSP
Employee contributionsUsually reduce taxable pay when madeMade from after-tax pay
Withdrawn contributionsGenerally taxable as incomeNot taxed again
Withdrawn investment earningsGenerally taxable as incomeTax-free only in a qualified distribution
Original owner's lifetime required minimum distributionsYes, when RMD rules applyNo

The table describes ordinary federal civilian TSP balances. Special treatment can apply to tax-exempt combat-zone contributions and inherited beneficiary accounts. Traditional TSP money generally has not been taxed before distribution; Roth contributions have. This is the basic distinction in IRS Publication 721. The TSP separately explains that the original owner's Roth balance is not subject to lifetime RMDs in its October 2025 RMD guidance.

What makes a Roth TSP withdrawal qualified?

A qualified Roth TSP distribution generally requires both (1) completion of the five-tax-year participation period and (2) a distribution after the participant reaches age 59½, dies, or meets the applicable disability definition. For the age condition, most retirees focus on 59½. The five-year clock generally starts on January 1 of the tax year in which the participant first made a designated Roth contribution to that plan, subject to special rollover rules.

For example, a participant whose first Roth TSP contribution was for tax year 2022 generally satisfies the five-tax-year requirement beginning January 1, 2027. That alone does not make the distribution qualified: the age, death or disability condition still matters. The clock is not simply five calendar years after the date of the first paycheck contribution. The IRS designated Roth FAQs explain the participation test and rollover exceptions.

If a Roth withdrawal is not qualified, it is not necessarily entirely tax-free. A nonqualified Roth distribution generally includes a proportional share of contributions and earnings. The contributions are not taxed again; the earnings portion may be included in taxable income and may face the 10% additional early-distribution tax unless an exception applies. See the IRS Roth distribution rules. Unlike Roth IRA contribution-ordering rules, you cannot assume all of a nonqualified Roth TSP withdrawal comes from contributions first.

Can you choose whether to withdraw traditional or Roth money?

Yes. The TSP says participants with both sources can choose a withdrawal from the traditional balance only, Roth balance only, or proportionally from both. That gives retirees some flexibility over the tax character of a withdrawal. See the TSP's withdrawal-source explanation and 5 CFR 1650.2(h).

Illustration: suppose a participant has $80,000 traditional and $20,000 Roth, with no other adjustments. A $2,000 proportional withdrawal would allocate roughly $1,600 to traditional and $400 to Roth based on those example balances. The traditional portion is generally taxable. Whether the Roth portion contains taxable earnings depends on the Roth qualified-distribution rules and the account's composition. Actual allocations depend on balances when processed. This is arithmetic, not a prediction of anyone's tax bill.

There is a second kind of proportional distribution: even when you select a source such as traditional, withdrawals are generally spread across the TSP investment funds held in the account. You cannot instruct the TSP to sell only your G Fund shares to make an ordinary withdrawal. This rule is in 5 CFR 1650.2(h). For investment-fund basics, read MillionFed's TSP funds explained guide.

What changed in 2026: Roth in-plan conversions

On January 28, 2026, the TSP announced that eligible participants could convert traditional TSP money into Roth TSP money inside their existing account. This includes active federal employees, separated and retired participants, and certain spouse beneficiary participants with eligible balances. The option is voluntary. It differs from making new Roth payroll contributions and from rolling traditional TSP money to an outside Roth IRA. The official TSP announcement provides the effective date and eligibility overview.

Converting pre-tax money generally increases taxable income in the year of conversion. The TSP states that taxes on the converted amount must be paid from other funds, not deducted from the conversion itself. A conversion can affect the current year's tax bracket and other income-sensitive costs. The TSP offers an in-plan conversion calculator and recommends professional tax advice. Neither the availability of the conversion nor a calculator result makes it a good choice for everyone.

Crucially, a conversion is not an RMD. If an RMD is due for the year, the TSP requires that obligation to be satisfied before an eligible conversion. The converted money may also be subject to separate five-year early-withdrawal consequences, while earnings must meet qualified-distribution rules to be tax-free. Do not confuse these separate tests. Some older IRS publications describe the pre-2026 TSP ban on in-plan Roth conversions; for current TSP availability, rely on the TSP's newer 2026 announcement.

Are RMDs required from both balances?

For an original-owner civilian TSP account, required minimum distributions apply to the traditional balance, not the Roth balance. The TSP says that for participants not already subject to RMDs in 2025, the RMD age is generally 73 if born before 1960 and 75 if born in 1960 or later. The applicable separation-from-service rules also matter. The first RMD may be due by April 1 of the following year, and subsequent annual RMDs are generally due by December 31. Beneficiary accounts have different rules.

A traditional-balance withdrawal during the year can count toward the traditional TSP RMD, but a Roth-balance withdrawal cannot satisfy it. An RMD also cannot be rolled over or converted to Roth. Read the TSP's official RMD explainer and verify your required beginning date with the TSP rather than assuming it starts on your retirement date.

Does retiring at 55 or 57 make Roth withdrawals tax-free?

No. The commonly called TSP rule of 55 may remove the additional 10% early-distribution tax for eligible employer-plan payments after separating during or after the calendar year you turn 55. But that exception does not erase ordinary income tax on traditional distributions or automatically satisfy the Roth five-year and age-59½ qualified-distribution conditions. A federal retiree can be eligible for the early-tax exception while still owing tax on traditional TSP money or nonqualified Roth earnings.

Read MillionFed's TSP rule-of-55 guide for separation-year timing, IRA rollover risks and special-category exceptions. The IRS describes the separate taxes in Publication 721. An ordinary USPS city carrier should not assume special public-safety employee exceptions apply.

USPS retirement: keep three questions separate

A USPS employee planning FERS retirement should distinguish (1) FERS pension eligibility, (2) when TSP money can be accessed, and (3) how a particular TSP payment is taxed. Qualifying for an unreduced FERS annuity at your minimum retirement age does not itself make a Roth TSP withdrawal qualified. The FERS Special Retirement Supplement is a separate benefit and does not change the TSP's Roth tax tests.

Coordinate the timing of your pension, any SRS and withdrawals before choosing how to fund the first retirement years. See MillionFed's FERS eligibility and SRS earnings-test guides. Individual service history, spouse rules, loans, beneficiary status and tax circumstances can change the answer.

What withdrawal forms can retirees use?

The TSP offers installments, partial distributions, a total distribution and the purchase of a life annuity through an outside provider. Installments can be adjusted while the remaining TSP balance stays invested, but they are not guaranteed to last for life. An annuity purchase trades control over the amount used to buy it for the contractual income stream. Different distributions have different withholding and rollover rules. Review the TSP's official overview rather than assuming every payment is treated the same.

Tax withholding is not your final tax bill. A distribution that has 20% withheld is not necessarily taxed at 20%. Actual tax liability depends on your total taxable income and filing circumstances. Consider the combined effect of your FERS pension, wages, Social Security, other investment income and TSP distributions when planning withdrawals.

Checklist before taking money out

  1. Check your traditional and Roth TSP balances separately.
  2. Confirm when your Roth five-tax-year clock began and whether the age/death/disability condition is met.
  3. Check whether a rule-of-55 or other early-distribution exception applies to the specific payment.
  4. Compare traditional-only, Roth-only and proportional withdrawal-source choices.
  5. Estimate the current-year tax cost before any 2026 in-plan Roth conversion and identify outside funds for tax payment.
  6. Verify whether an RMD is due; satisfy it before a conversion or rollover.
  7. Review spouse, loan, withholding and rollover requirements with the TSP and a qualified tax professional.
