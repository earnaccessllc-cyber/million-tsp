TSP Rule of 55: When Federal Employees Can Withdraw Without the 10% Penalty

Federal employees who retire before age 59½ often wonder whether they can withdraw from the Thrift Savings Plan without a 10% early-withdrawal penalty. The answer depends on when they separate from federal service, not just the age when they take the money. This guide explains the calendar-year test, taxes, IRA rollover risks, and what regular FERS and USPS employees should know.

Quick answer: If you separate from federal service during or after the calendar year you turn 55, subsequent eligible TSP distributions generally avoid the IRS's additional 10% early-distribution tax. Separating in an earlier calendar year does not qualify merely because you wait until 55 to withdraw. Ordinary income tax may still apply. See IRS Publication 721 and IRS Publication 575.

What exactly is the rule of 55?

The “rule of 55” is the common name for an exception to the additional tax on early distributions from certain employer retirement plans, including the TSP. Normally, taxable distributions received before age 59½ can trigger an additional 10% tax. The separation-from-service exception removes that extra tax when the timing requirement is met. It is not a separate FERS pension eligibility rule and does not guarantee tax-free income. The IRS discusses the additional tax and its exceptions in Topic 558.

Keep two questions separate: whether TSP plan rules permit a withdrawal, and whether that withdrawal qualifies for an exception to the IRS additional tax. A distribution can be allowed yet still taxable. You should also consider whether taking money early fits a sustainable retirement spending plan.

Why the year you leave matters more than your birthday

For this exception, the IRS focuses on the calendar year you turn 55. You can generally separate earlier in that same calendar year, before your birthday, and meet the timing test. But if you leave in the previous calendar year, waiting until 55 to take the distribution generally does not fix the problem. IRS Publication 575 gives an example of a worker who separated at 49 and later withdrew at 55; the age-55 exception did not apply.

ExampleGeneral age-55 exception result
Turn 55 in October 2027; leave federal service in February 2027Generally qualifies for later eligible TSP distributions
Turn 55 in October 2027; leave in December 2026Does not qualify merely by waiting until 2027
Leave federal service at age 57Generally satisfies the age-55 separation timing test
Still employed at age 55Separation exception does not apply while still employed; separate in-service rules may apply

These are ordinary civilian federal-service examples. Other exceptions may apply, and the IRS determines individual tax treatment.

Does the rule of 55 make a TSP withdrawal tax-free?

No. The exception removes a possible additional 10% tax; it does not remove ordinary federal income tax on taxable traditional TSP withdrawals. Traditional TSP distributions generally count as taxable income unless properly rolled over. Withholding is a prepayment of tax, not necessarily your final tax liability. IRS Publication 721 explains TSP cash withdrawals and withholding.

Consider a simplified example: a $20,000 fully taxable traditional TSP withdrawal before 59½ could incur an additional $2,000 tax (10% of $20,000) if no exception applies, on top of ordinary income tax. If the age-55 separation exception applies, that extra $2,000 generally does not apply, but the $20,000 can still be taxable income. This is an illustration, not an estimate of anyone's personal taxes.

Why an IRA rollover can change the result

The age-55 separation exception is generally available for qualifying employer-plan distributions, not distributions from an IRA. A federal employee who separates at 57 may qualify for penalty-exempt TSP withdrawals. If the person first rolls that money into a traditional IRA, a later IRA withdrawal before 59½ generally does not get the same age-55 exception. Other IRA exceptions may exist, but they should not be assumed. The IRS Form 5329 instructions, exception 01, explicitly distinguish employer plans from IRAs.

A properly executed direct rollover itself generally does not create a taxable cash distribution. The potential problem is a subsequent withdrawal from the IRA. Compare fees, investment choices, beneficiary arrangements, access needs, and tax rules before moving TSP money—especially if you may need income before age 59½.

What if you have a Roth TSP balance?

Roth TSP contributions are after-tax money, but Roth earnings are tax-free only when the distribution satisfies the qualified-distribution rules. In general, those rules require meeting a five-year period and an eligible condition such as reaching age 59½, death, or disability. A withdrawal at 57 might qualify for the age-55 exception to the additional 10% tax yet still include taxable Roth earnings because the Roth distribution is not qualified. Review IRS Publication 721's Roth TSP discussion and the official TSP retirement tax explainer.

Traditional and Roth balances have different tax histories. Avoid treating all Roth withdrawals as automatically tax-free or all traditional withdrawals as automatically subject to the additional penalty.

Do qualified public safety employees have a different rule?

Yes. Certain statutorily qualified public safety employees may qualify for an exception after separation during or after the year they reach age 50, or when they meet an applicable 25-year service condition, whichever is earlier. The IRS identifies specific groups, including qualifying federal law enforcement officers, federal firefighters, air traffic controllers and other defined occupations. A physically demanding federal job does not automatically qualify. IRS Publication 575 discusses these special provisions.

USPS letter carriers and FERS retirement at 57

A regular USPS city letter carrier generally follows the standard age-55 separation exception, not the special public-safety rule. Someone who retires from regular FERS-covered postal employment at 57 generally meets the TSP age-55 timing test for eligible distributions. But FERS pension eligibility, the FERS Special Retirement Supplement (SRS), and TSP withdrawal taxes are separate systems. The TSP exception does not create eligibility for a pension or SRS.

For the age-and-service requirements, read MillionFed's FERS retirement eligibility guide. For the separate supplement earnings test, see the SRS earnings-test guide. Verify your personal retirement provision and separation records with USPS and OPM.

What if you separated before the year you turned 55?

Waiting until your 55th birthday generally does not make that earlier separation qualify. The normal age-59½ threshold, or another specific exception, may eliminate the additional tax later. Certain substantially equal periodic payments, disability-related payments, and other circumstances have their own exceptions. These rules are technical and not interchangeable; modifying substantially equal periodic payments improperly can trigger recapture tax. See IRS guidance on substantially equal periodic payments.

What if the TSP tax form does not show the exception?

The TSP reports distributions on Form 1099-R, but an applicable exception may need to be claimed on a tax return when the distribution code does not reflect it. IRS Form 5329 instructions explain the process. Keep records of your birth date, separation date, TSP withdrawals and any rollovers. Complex employment histories, Roth distributions and IRA transfers are good reasons to obtain individualized tax advice.

Checklist before taking TSP money between 55 and 59½

  1. Confirm the calendar year of separation and the year you turn 55.
  2. Determine whether the ordinary or qualified public safety exception applies.
  3. Identify whether the money is in the TSP or an IRA.
  4. Separate ordinary income tax from the possible 10% additional tax.
  5. Check the Roth five-year and qualified-distribution requirements when relevant.
  6. Review TSP withdrawal options, withholding, spousal requirements and outstanding loans.
  7. Coordinate withdrawals with your FERS pension, SRS and retirement spending plan.
