Unused Sick Leave Under FERS: What Counts Toward Your Pension?

You have banked hundreds—or perhaps thousands—of hours of federal sick leave. Will those hours help you retire earlier, increase your monthly FERS pension, or simply disappear? The answer depends on the difference between retirement eligibility and annuity computation. Those are not the same thing.

Bottom line: For qualifying FERS retirements beginning in 2014 or later, OPM generally credits 100% of unused sick leave toward the service time used to calculate an immediate annuity. Sick leave does not help you satisfy the minimum service required to qualify for retirement. OPM does not simply issue a cash payout for the unused hours. See OPM's FERS creditable-service rules and its sick-leave retirement FAQ.

What happens to unused sick leave when you retire under FERS?

If you separate for an eligible immediate FERS retirement, your agency reports the unused sick-leave balance to OPM. OPM converts eligible hours into additional years, months and days of service for the pension calculation. The extra credit can increase the gross basic annuity, but it does not create additional years of employment, change your high-3 salary or add money to your TSP.

For FERS annuity entitlements based on separations on or after January 1, 2014, the full eligible sick-leave balance counts for this computation. The earlier 50% rule applied to qualifying separations from October 28, 2009 through December 31, 2013; it is not the rule for a normal 2026 retirement. The rules for an employee with a CSRS component or unusual service history may require separate review. Source: OPM.

Can sick leave make you eligible to retire sooner?

No. This is the most important distinction. Suppose a regular FERS employee has reached their minimum retirement age (MRA) but has only 29 years and 8 months of actual qualifying service. Four months of unused sick leave cannot turn that into the MRA + 30 retirement eligibility requirement. The employee must first meet the age-and-service eligibility test without relying on unused sick leave.

Likewise, someone with 19 years of service at age 60 cannot use unused sick leave to qualify for the age 60 + 20 immediate retirement provision. The hours may matter after eligibility is independently established. Review MillionFed's FERS retirement eligibility guide and OPM's eligibility table.

How many sick-leave hours equal one year of service?

OPM uses a 2,087-hour work year for the standard sick-leave conversion chart. That means 2,087 eligible unused sick-leave hours represent one year of service credit for annuity computation—not the 2,080 hours many employees associate with 52 standard 40-hour workweeks. For employees on a standard tour, the OPM chart converts hours into years, months and days. Employees on uncommon tours of duty may need a different calculation from HR. OPM CSRS/FERS Handbook, Chapter 50, sick-leave conversion chart.

Unused sick leaveWhat it means
0 hoursNo extra sick-leave service credit.
About 1,000 hoursRoughly half a year of potential service credit; use the official chart for exact months and days.
2,087 hoursOne additional year of computation credit under the standard conversion.
More than 2,087 hoursMore than one year of computation credit; the exact result follows OPM conversion rules.

Watch the leftover days. OPM combines creditable service and sick-leave credit and then uses completed years and months in the annuity calculation. Odd days that do not complete a month are dropped. This is why a precise official conversion can differ from simply dividing your sick-leave hours by 2,087 and treating the decimal as the final pension service figure.

Example: How much more pension can one year of sick leave add?

Consider a regular FERS employee who already qualifies for an immediate retirement at age 57 with 30 years of actual service, has a $90,000 high-3, and retires with 2,087 hours of eligible unused sick leave. Ignore other adjustments for this educational illustration.

CalculationGross annual basic annuity
Without sick leave: $90,000 × 30 × 1%$27,000
With one year of sick leave: $90,000 × 31 × 1%$27,900
Difference$900 a year, or $75 a month

The example uses the standard 1% FERS multiplier because the employee retires before age 62. It shows gross pension amounts before taxes, survivor elections, insurance premiums or other deductions. It does not mean the retiree receives a $90,000 cash payout for their sick leave. For the underlying formula, see OPM's FERS computation page and MillionFed's pension formula guide.

Can sick leave help you qualify for the 1.1% FERS pension multiplier?

Sometimes—this is a different test from retirement eligibility. For a regular FERS employee who is at least age 62 at separation, OPM's 1.1% annuity formula applies when the computation service total reaches 20 years. OPM issued a specific clarification explaining that someone who is already eligible to retire, has less than 20 years of actual service, but reaches 20 years after eligible unused sick leave is added, can qualify for the 1.1% computation factor. OPM Benefits Administration Letter 18-103.

For example, an age-62 employee with 19 years and 6 months of actual service and at least 6 months of eligible sick-leave credit could reach 20 years for annuity computation. That does not change the rule that unused sick leave cannot make an otherwise ineligible employee eligible for retirement. Verify the official conversion and full service record with HR.

Does unused sick leave count for the FERS Special Retirement Supplement?

Do not assume the additional sick-leave months will make you eligible for the FERS annuity supplement (SRS). The supplement has separate retirement eligibility and calculation rules. Sick leave cannot convert an MRA+10 retirement into an MRA+30 retirement. The SRS is not payable for MRA+10 or deferred retirements. See OPM's supplement eligibility rules and MillionFed's supplement earnings-test guide. Ask your agency to confirm any case-specific supplement computation.

What about deferred retirement or leaving federal service before eligibility?

The standard unused-sick-leave annuity credit discussed here is tied to a qualifying immediate annuity or qualifying death-in-service survivor computation. If you leave federal employment and later claim a deferred FERS pension, do not assume unused sick leave will be credited to that deferred pension. This is another reason to understand your separation category before resigning. Read MillionFed's postponed versus deferred retirement comparison and request a written estimate from your agency.

USPS employees: what should you check?

USPS career employees covered by regular FERS generally need to distinguish the same two questions: Have I met retirement eligibility with actual qualifying service? and How much unused sick leave will increase my annuity computation? Postal Service Health Benefits (PSHB), TSP withdrawals and any annuity supplement are separate decisions. Special retirement provisions, workers' compensation periods, uncommon tours or mixed CSRS/FERS records can affect an individual case; request a USPS retirement estimate rather than using the standard example as your official figure.

Should you save sick leave or use it before retirement?

Sick leave exists for legitimate medical and qualifying family needs. A potential pension increase is one factor in planning, but not a reason to work while ill or to avoid needed care. The financial comparison also depends on your high-3, retirement date, service rounding and benefit elections. Federal sick leave generally has no accumulation cap, but rules governing legitimate use still apply. OPM: sick-leave accrual and use.

Checklist before your retirement application

  1. Ask HR for your verified actual creditable service and the retirement provision you qualify for without unused sick leave.
  2. Get the official unused sick-leave hours that will be certified at separation.
  3. Ask for the OPM chart conversion into additional years, months and days, including any unusual tour or CSRS component.
  4. Request pension estimates with and without sick-leave credit, including the correct 1% or 1.1% formula.
  5. Confirm FEHB or USPS PSHB, survivor benefits, SRS eligibility and TSP decisions separately.
