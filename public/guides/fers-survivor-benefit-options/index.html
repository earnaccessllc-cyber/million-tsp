FERS Survivor Benefits: Full vs. Partial Spouse Annuity

A FERS pension can provide income for your lifetime—but what happens to your spouse after you die? At retirement, eligible married federal employees generally choose between a full survivor annuity, a partial survivor annuity, or no spouse survivor annuity. That choice changes the retiree's monthly pension and can determine whether a surviving spouse can continue federal health coverage. This guide explains the numbers, the consent requirement, and the extra considerations for USPS retirees.

Quick answer: Under ordinary FERS retirement rules, the full spouse survivor annuity is 50% of your unreduced basic annuity and costs a 10% reduction in your own annuity. The partial option pays 25% and costs a 5% reduction. A no-survivor election generally has no survivor reduction, but a married employee needs spouse consent to choose less than the full benefit. Source: OPM Survivor Benefits.

What are the three FERS spouse survivor choices?

The survivor election is part of the federal retirement application, not a separate TSP investment decision. The percentages apply to the retiree's earned basic FERS annuity before the survivor reduction. The spouse's future payment is not 50% or 25% of the already-reduced check. Other adjustments and circumstances can affect actual payments, so use your agency's estimate and OPM's final determination.

Election at retirementRetiree's basic annuity reductionSpouse's survivor annuity after retiree's death
Full survivor10%50% of unreduced earned basic annuity
Partial survivor5%25% of unreduced earned basic annuity
No survivorNo survivor reductionNo spouse survivor annuity based on this election

OPM explains the options in its survivor benefits overview and FERS computation guidance. The ordinary 50%/25% choices are different from the 55% maximum survivor benefit used in the older CSRS system.

Example: What if your FERS pension is $36,000 a year?

Assume a hypothetical retiree has an unreduced FERS basic annuity of $36,000 per year, or $3,000 per month, before the survivor election. This simplified comparison excludes taxes, insurance premiums, COLAs, other reductions and special circumstances. It is not a personal pension quote.

ElectionRetiree's basic annuity after survivor reductionSurviving spouse's annual basic annuity
Full: 10% reduction$32,400/year ($2,700/month)$18,000/year ($1,500/month)
Partial: 5% reduction$34,200/year ($2,850/month)$9,000/year ($750/month)
None$36,000/year ($3,000/month)$0 under this election

The practical tradeoff is income now versus income protection later. The full option costs this hypothetical retiree $300 per month compared with no survivor election; the partial option costs $150 per month. The spouse's future annuity is based on the unreduced benefit, not on the $2,700 or $2,850 monthly checks. Actual survivor payments may reflect applicable adjustments over time.

Does your spouse have to agree to a smaller benefit?

Generally, yes. If you are married when you retire under FERS, OPM normally requires a full spouse survivor election unless your spouse consents to a partial or no-survivor election. This protects the spouse from an election that would otherwise remove or reduce future income and potentially affect health benefits. The FERS retirement application includes the spouse-consent process; OPM Form SF 3107 includes the related instructions and consent form.

Do not assume you can simply select the highest current pension on a calculator without the spouse's participation. Special circumstances, including inability to locate a spouse or court-ordered former-spouse benefits, have specific documentation requirements. Work with your agency's retirement counselor well before your planned separation date.

Why FEHB and USPS PSHB coverage make the decision bigger

Survivor elections are not just about a monthly pension. For a spouse who is covered as a family member under a retiring federal employee's Federal Employees Health Benefits (FEHB) enrollment, eligibility to continue that coverage after the retiree dies generally depends on receiving a survivor annuity and meeting other program requirements. OPM warns that choosing no survivor annuity can cause coverage through the deceased retiree to end. See OPM's retirement planning guidance.

For USPS letter carriers and other postal employees, the relevant health program is normally Postal Service Health Benefits (PSHB), not a standard FEHB plan. PSHB became the applicable program for eligible Postal Service employees and annuitants in 2025. OPM states that a surviving spouse or survivor annuitant may be eligible to continue PSHB and that the survivor's eligibility follows the same rules as FEHB. Check enrollment history, survivor-annuity eligibility and any Medicare Part B rules rather than assuming coverage is automatic. See OPM's PSHB program guidance.

A spouse may have independent health coverage or other options, but that does not make the survivor election irrelevant. Compare the value of ongoing health coverage alongside the pension income, not as an afterthought. Ask your retirement office for a written explanation of how your specific election affects your spouse.

What if you are divorced or have a former spouse?

A former spouse can sometimes be entitled to a FERS survivor annuity through a qualifying court order or an election. This can limit the amount available to a current spouse. OPM notes that the maximum combined survivor benefits for current and former spouses generally cannot exceed 50% of the unreduced basic annuity. The treatment of court orders is technical, and simply naming a new spouse on a retirement application does not necessarily override a prior order.

If your marital history includes divorce, remarriage, or a court-ordered survivor benefit, obtain a review of the actual order and the election forms before retirement. OPM's survivor benefits page explains the interactions between current-spouse and former-spouse elections.

What if you marry after you retire?

A FERS retiree who marries after retirement may be able to elect a survivor annuity for the new spouse, but OPM generally requires the election within two years of the marriage. This is not necessarily priced like an election made at retirement: OPM describes the regular survivor reduction plus an additional actuarial reduction relating to the period before the election became effective. Do not assume you can add a new spouse at the same cost years later. Read OPM's post-retirement marriage FAQ and contact OPM promptly after a qualifying life event.

Does a survivor election affect TSP beneficiaries?

The FERS survivor annuity and the Thrift Savings Plan are separate retirement benefits. Electing a full spouse survivor annuity does not itself decide who receives your TSP account after death. Keep your TSP beneficiary information current through the official TSP account process, and separately review any applicable spousal protections for TSP withdrawals. Do not confuse a FERS survivor election with an IRA or life-insurance beneficiary designation.

For related retirement planning, see MillionFed's TSP rule-of-55 guide and FERS pension calculation guide. Your FERS pension estimate is only one part of the household income picture.

Does the FERS Special Retirement Supplement pass to a spouse?

The regular FERS Special Retirement Supplement (SRS) is a benefit tied to an eligible retiree's own circumstances before age 62; it is not simply inherited as the same monthly supplement. A surviving spouse's benefits have separate rules, and certain survivor supplements may apply under specific circumstances. Do not use the retiree's SRS amount as an estimate of the surviving spouse's income. Ask OPM for an individualized survivor estimate. MillionFed's SRS earnings-test guide explains a different issue: how earnings can affect the retiree's own supplement.

Checklist before signing your retirement application

  1. Request your agency's unreduced basic FERS annuity estimate and each survivor-option estimate.
  2. Compare the household budget with the 10%, 5%, and no-survivor reductions.
  3. Discuss the election and any required spouse consent before the application deadline.
  4. Verify whether your spouse's future FEHB or PSHB continuation depends on the election.
  5. Review divorce decrees, former-spouse orders and any remarriage considerations.
  6. Check TSP beneficiaries, life insurance and other survivor resources separately.
  7. Retain copies of retirement forms and obtain OPM confirmation of the final election.
