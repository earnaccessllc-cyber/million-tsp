TSP funds explained

What each of the 16 Thrift Savings Plan funds holds, how risky it is, and how federal employees put them together.

Updated October 27, 2026 · MillionFed Guides

The Thrift Savings Plan offers 16 funds: five individual funds (G, F, C, S and I) and eleven Lifecycle (L) funds that mix those five for you. Every TSP fund is a low-cost index fund, so the real decision is how much risk you want, not which manager to pick.

The five individual funds at a glance

FundWhat it holdsRisk
G FundSpecial-issue U.S. Treasury securities sold only to the TSPLowest: principal cannot go down
F FundU.S. investment-grade bonds (Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Bond Index)Low to moderate
C FundLarge U.S. companies (S&P 500 Index)Moderate to high
S FundSmall and mid-size U.S. companies not in the S&P 500High
I FundInternational stocks in developed and emerging markets outside the U.S.High

G Fund: the safe harbor

The G Fund holds government securities issued specifically to the TSP. Its interest rate is set monthly from the average yield on long-term U.S. Treasury securities, and the government guarantees you will not lose principal. The trade-off is that over long periods its return has often barely outpaced inflation. Many federal employees use it for money they will need in the next few years.

F Fund: bonds

The F Fund tracks a broad index of U.S. government, mortgage and corporate bonds. Bond prices fall when interest rates rise, so the F Fund can lose value in a year like 2022. Over time it has usually returned more than the G Fund, with more ups and downs.

C Fund: the S&P 500

The C Fund tracks the S&P 500, about 500 of the largest U.S. companies. It is the most popular stock fund in the TSP and historically one of its strongest long-term performers, but it has had sharp drops, including about 37% in 2008.

S Fund: small and mid caps

The S Fund owns U.S. companies that are not in the S&P 500. Pairing it with the C Fund gives you nearly the entire U.S. stock market. Smaller companies tend to swing more than large ones, in both directions.

I Fund: international

The I Fund invests in companies outside the United States across developed and emerging markets (excluding China and Hong Kong under the current benchmark). It adds diversification, because foreign and U.S. markets do not always move together, and it is also affected by currency changes.

L Funds: set it and forget it

The Lifecycle funds are target-date funds. Each one, from L 2030 through L 2075, holds a mix of the G, F, C, S and I funds that gets gradually more conservative as its target year approaches. L Income is for people already withdrawing. You pick the fund closest to the year you expect to need the money, and the TSP rebalances for you.

Default fund: New federal employees who never choose are invested in the L Fund closest to the year they turn 62.

How to choose a mix

  • Decades from retirement: many investors lean heavily on stock funds (C, S, I) because they have time to ride out drops.
  • Within 5 to 10 years: adding G and F lowers the chance that a crash lands right before you retire.
  • Not sure: an L Fund makes these decisions automatically.

TSP share prices are calculated after each business day's market close, and changes you make through an interfund transfer take effect at that day's closing price if submitted before the noon Eastern cutoff.
